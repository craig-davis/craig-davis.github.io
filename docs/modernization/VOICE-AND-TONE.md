# There4 Voice and Tone

This guide describes Craig Davis's established editorial voice and how to preserve it during review or AI-assisted drafting. It is descriptive, not aspirational: the patterns come from the existing archive across machines, making, software, books, and reflective writing.

## The central voice

There4 reads like field notes from a capable, curious person who actually did the work.

The voice is:

- **Firsthand.** It begins with what Craig owned, noticed, tried, broke, repaired, read, or learned.
- **Practical.** Details exist to help someone make a decision or complete a task.
- **Conversational.** Contractions, direct address, and occasional dry humor keep technical material human.
- **Specific.** Parts, dimensions, fitment, tools, failures, and changes of plan matter.
- **Reflective.** The best articles explain not only what worked, but why the decision made sense.
- **Generous.** The reader gets the inconvenient details, alternatives, warnings, and lessons—not merely the polished result.
- **Modest about certainty.** Craig distinguishes observation, preference, inference, and verified fact.

The site is not a magazine speaking from nowhere, a generic buying guide, a corporate knowledge base, or an optimized-content machine.

## The recurring narrative shape

Many strong There4 articles follow this movement:

1. **A concrete situation.** A line interfered with a winch, a visor pin broke, a tool made repetitive work easier, or a decision framework created a useful analogy.
2. **The available choices.** Craig explains the smaller repair, common approach, default assumption, or tool he could have used.
3. **The chosen path.** The article names the tradeoff and why this approach fit the project.
4. **Evidence from the work.** Photographs, parts, code, books, measurements, and observations carry the article.
5. **A result with elapsed experience.** “I’ve been running these…” is stronger than declaring success immediately after assembly.
6. **A useful qualification.** Fitment, safety, uncertainty, an imperfect photo, an unavailable product, or a better next attempt remains visible.

Do not force this structure on a short essay, but preserve its underlying honesty: situation, reasoning, evidence, outcome.

## Point of view

### Use “I” for evidence and judgment

Use first person when the claim comes from Craig's experience:

- what he bought or built
- what he observed
- why he chose one approach
- what surprised him
- what he would change
- how long the result has been in use

“I found,” “I used,” “I tried,” and “I like” are strengths when they identify the source of the claim. Do not edit them into detached authority.

### Use “you” for the reader's decision

Address the reader directly when offering a check, warning, or practical option. Avoid pretending every reader has the same vehicle, shop, body, terrain, or tolerance for risk.

Prefer:

> Check which flow direction your Jeep actually uses before making the hoses.

Over:

> Simply install the fittings as shown.

### Use “we” only when the article truly shares a sequence

Instructional prose can use “we” to move through a repair, but it should not become a synthetic tutorial voice. Craig's natural writing usually remains grounded in “I” and then offers the result to “you.”

## Sentence and paragraph rhythm

- Favor clear sentences with one primary job.
- Mix concise statements with a longer explanatory sentence when the reasoning matters.
- Keep paragraphs focused on one observation, choice, or stage of work.
- Use contractions naturally: “I’ve,” “don’t,” “it’s,” and “you’ll.”
- Allow a restrained exclamation when the enthusiasm is earned; do not salt every paragraph with one.
- Parentheses are useful for small qualifications, not for hiding the main point.
- Lists and tables should carry real reference value, not merely break up prose.

Craig's voice can be informal without being careless. Correct spelling, grammar, duplicated words, and ambiguous pronouns while keeping the cadence and vocabulary recognizable.

## Diction

Prefer ordinary, concrete language:

- “fits,” “rubs,” “seized,” “fiddly,” “held pressure,” “stays out of the way”
- “I didn't like how the lines were situated”
- “This is a hobby, and so I went all-in”
- “My photos of the bottom aren't great”

Avoid generic editorial filler:

- “In today's fast-paced world”
- “Whether you're a beginner or a seasoned professional”
- “game-changing,” “revolutionary,” “ultimate,” or “must-have” without concrete evidence
- “delve,” “unlock,” “elevate,” “seamless,” and other AI-default abstractions
- conclusions that merely repeat the introduction

Enthusiasm should attach to an observed benefit: 53 drawer boxes without shaking a glue bottle is persuasive; “an incredible solution” is not.

## Titles, decks, and descriptions

### Titles

Name the object, problem, or idea directly. Specific nouns outperform clever vagueness.

Established patterns include:

- object + work: “Jeep XJ Transmission Cooler Line Upgrade”
- problem + solution: “Repairing a Smith Wilder Jr Helmet Visor”
- opinion grounded in use: “Shop Tools I Wish I'd Bought Sooner”
- conceptual bridge: “Skiing, Snow, and Software”
- story title: “The Legend of the Aged Shredder”

A clever title may lead, but the subtitle or description must explain the actual value.

### Subtitles or decks

Add the missing promise or constraint. Do not repeat the title using synonyms.

### Descriptions

Write one factual sentence describing what the article gives the reader. Mention the relevant object and useful outcome, not a sales pitch. Descriptions are metadata, but they should still sound like There4.

## Machines and repair writing

Machine articles earn trust through fitment and consequences.

Include when applicable:

- year, model, configuration, and meaningful variations
- exact parts and quantities
- tool choices and substitutes
- torque, pressure, clearance, routing, or dimensions with sources
- what did not work
- safety boundaries
- how long the result has been used
- what would be checked differently next time

Never generalize one vehicle or component into universal compatibility. Acknowledge confusing manuals, model-year changes, and uncertain evidence plainly.

Safety language should be calm and unmistakable. Do not bury a serious risk in playful prose, and do not overstate expertise.

## Making and tool writing

Tool recommendations should answer:

- What recurring annoyance did this remove?
- What actual project proved useful?
- How does it live in the shop or bag?
- What is the limitation or cheaper alternative?
- Has it survived enough use to recommend?

Small tools are interesting because of the work they enable. The article should not become a catalog. A pencil, flat bar, bit holder, or beater chisel earns space through a story of use.

## Software writing

Begin with the cognitive or team problem, then show code.

- Explain behavior before praising a pattern.
- Use progressive examples when each revision teaches a distinct idea.
- Name the mental work reduced by a refactor.
- Prefer a believable constraint over a toy abstraction.
- Admit when an example is simplified or still a draft.
- Treat source control, review, debugging, and architecture as human communication as well as mechanics.

Do not modernize old code examples silently. Add an update note or a new section that explains the contemporary approach and preserves the historical context.

## Reading and reflective writing

Book recommendations should disclose the relationship: when Craig read the book, what stayed with him, who might enjoy it, and where it falls short. Plot-summary language alone does not sound like There4.

Reflective pieces work best when anchored in a scene—the chairlift, trailhead, workshop, or engineering meeting—before widening into the lesson. Let a concrete detail carry emotion. Avoid announcing profundity.

Humor is dry, occasional, and usually self-directed. It should release pressure, not undercut safety or another person.

## Photography

Photographs are evidence, memory, and context. The prose should tell the reader why a photo matters without narrating every visible object.

For a photo-driven story:

- establish place and time
- identify people only with appropriate context and permission
- sequence wide context, action, and detail intentionally
- distinguish remembered history from documented fact
- say when the photographs are old, incomplete, or technically imperfect
- do not invent what happened outside the surviving record

## Recommendations, products, and affiliate links

The editorial sequence is:

1. firsthand use
2. useful evidence
3. limitations or fit
4. recommendation
5. optional merchant destination

Never reverse that sequence to begin with a commission opportunity.

Use “I recommend” only when Craig can defend the claim from real use. Avoid ranking products that were not tested. Do not infer current quality from an older model without saying so. Clearly disclose affiliate destinations and keep purchase language quieter than the field notes.

Visually distinctive product callouts should be rare. Their prominence must reflect unusually strong firsthand evidence, not merchant payout.

## Uncertainty, corrections, and updates

Different kinds of statements should sound different:

- **Observed:** “My hoses held pressure overnight.”
- **Preference:** “I like the 45-degree fitting here.”
- **Inference:** “It appears Jeep changed the flow direction in 1997.”
- **Recommendation:** “Document your routing before removing the old lines.”
- **Unknown:** “I could not confirm whether every model used the same valve.”

Do not collapse these into the same confident voice.

When updating an article, preserve the original experience and label meaningful new information. Use `lastmod` only for a substantial revision. If a recommendation has aged, explain what changed rather than pretending the old article was written today.

## AI-assisted drafting rules

AI may help organize notes, identify missing questions, tighten repetition, propose headings, or create a clearly marked first draft. It must not manufacture Craig's experience.

Never invent:

- ownership, use, duration, or test results
- a fitment detail, measurement, torque value, or specification
- a failure, lesson, conversation, or emotional reaction
- a photograph, setting, date, or sequence of events
- a product comparison Craig did not perform
- a source Craig did not review

When evidence is missing, leave an explicit drafting marker such as:

```text
[CRAIG: How long has this bag been in use, and what has failed or worn?]
```

AI-generated prose should be treated as a question-shaped draft. Craig supplies the observations and decides the claim.

## Editorial review pass

### Voice

- Does the opening contain a real situation rather than generic setup?
- Can the reader tell which claims come from Craig's experience?
- Does the prose sound natural when read aloud?
- Did editing remove useful personality, uncertainty, or humor?
- Are enthusiastic words supported by a concrete reason?

### Usefulness

- Are configuration, fitment, tools, parts, and prerequisites clear?
- Are failed attempts and changed decisions included when useful?
- Does each image teach, prove, or establish context?
- Are the result and elapsed use described honestly?
- Can the reader tell whether the recommendation applies to them?

### Trust

- Are facts, observations, preferences, and inferences distinguished?
- Are safety limits prominent and proportional?
- Are sources linked for specifications or consequential claims?
- Are affiliate relationships clear?
- Are old details preserved or explicitly updated?

### Restraint

- Remove generic introductions, repeated conclusions, and SEO filler.
- Remove headings that contain only one disposable sentence.
- Remove products that have no firsthand role in the article.
- Remove adjectives that are doing the work of missing evidence.
